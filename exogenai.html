ExoGenAI: a hybrid exoplanet detector for real telescope data

A hybrid exoplanet detection system that pairs the Box Least Squares algorithm with a CNN-Transformer classifier — started as a NASA Space Apps Challenge entry, now being rebuilt to handle raw telescope data instead of pre-cleaned datasets.

NASA Space Apps 2025

The problem

ExoGenAI analyzes stellar light curves from space telescopes to classify confirmed exoplanets, candidates, and false positives — with enough transparency that an astronomer can see why the model made its call, not just trust a black box.

It started as a winning entry at NASA's Space Apps Challenge 2025 and has since become an ongoing effort to turn that prototype into a tool that fits real astronomical workflows. I handle model training and the backend; teammates handle frontend integration and presentation.

Architecture

The core idea is hybrid by design: the Box Least Squares (BLS) algorithm does physics-based pre-filtering before a CNN-Transformer network — 2.5 million parameters — handles classification. Pairing a physics-grounded filter with a learned model was a deliberate choice to keep the system interpretable rather than just accurate.

The backend runs on FastAPI, with Lightkurve handling astronomical data preprocessing ahead of the model.

The hackathon build placed strongly enough to earn three awards at Space Apps 2025. The honest caveat: it was trained on just over 5,000 samples from a single pre-processed dataset, which is nowhere near enough for real telescope observations — and that gap became the next phase of the project.

Rebuilding on raw data

Pre-processed, competition-ready data produces a model that doesn't survive contact with real telescope output — raw data needs its own preprocessing pipeline that a cleaned Kaggle dataset can't teach you. That realization drove a pivot toward Lightkurve, the NASA Exoplanet Archive, and the MAST Archive as raw data sources, with a goal of scaling training data past 50,000 samples across Kepler-confirmed planets, false positives, candidates, and TESS mission data.

What actually mattered

  • Astronomical data is genuinely, not artificially, imbalanced — 99%+ false positives — which called for weighted cross-entropy loss rather than more data cleaning.
  • The hybrid approach earns its complexity: physics-based validation plus deep learning gives both performance and the interpretability real astronomical workflows need.
  • Polished hackathon documentation and the underlying codebase diverge more than expected — worth auditing what actually exists before planning the next phase.
  • Since NASA data is public domain, open-sourcing the model is confirmed to be legally viable.
