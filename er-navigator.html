ER Navigator: predicting wait times across 20 GTHA hospitals

A stacking ensemble that predicts emergency room wait times at 30, 60, and 120-minute horizons across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area, built as a capstone project and pushed into a running production system.

COMP 385 Capstone

The problem

ER Navigator predicts emergency room wait times at 30, 60, and 120-minute horizons for 20 hospitals across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area. The target was ambitious going in: a mean absolute error under 30 minutes at the 60-minute horizon, with at least a 20% improvement over baseline methods.

Built with a team of five — Jaekyeong, Miguel, Varun, Younghun, and myself — under the supervision of Professor Mayy Habayeb, integrating live data from the ER Watch API alongside Environment Canada weather feeds.

Architecture

The core model is a 5-model stacking ensemble — LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, Quantile Random Forest, and LightGBM Quantile — feeding an XGBoost meta-learner. 47 engineered features span temporal patterns, lag values, rolling statistics, hospital-specific aggregates, and weather.

What started as proof-of-concept modeling became a full production system: PostgreSQL for storage, FastAPI serving predictions, and a React frontend with interactive maps (react-leaflet) showing live wait-time estimates. The backend scrapes ER Watch every 15 minutes and layers conformal prediction on top of the point forecasts, giving 80% and 95% confidence intervals instead of a single misleading number.

The final numbers beat the original target by 18%. Stress testing — after moving to a multi-worker Uvicorn setup with connection pooling — held a 99.98% success rate at 36+ requests per second, with an average response time of 33.8 milliseconds.

What actually mattered

  • Tree-based models beat classical time-series approaches by a wide margin — neural nets performed 4.3× worse than the tree ensemble.
  • Weather features were paradoxical: they hurt individual models but helped once the meta-learner combined everything.
  • Data quality was the real bottleneck. Only 21.73% of intervals landed within ±5 seconds of the expected 15-minute spacing, which meant resampling before any model would behave.
  • Temporal and lag features dominated feature importance — recent hospital state predicts near-term wait times better than almost anything else.
  • A simple persistence baseline is deceptively strong for short-horizon ER forecasting, since wait times are highly autocorrelated over 60 minutes.
